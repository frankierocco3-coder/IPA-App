# Speech Dissection — product specification

**Status: specification, not a build order.** Nothing here is scheduled. Build prompts
reference this document section by section. Keeping the framework here rather than in a
prompt is deliberate: a coding agent given 5,000 words of philosophy will build 5,000
words of philosophy.

Companion documents:

- `THRESHOLD_COPY.md` — verbatim copy for Before You Speak
- `BUILD_01_BEFORE_YOU_SPEAK.md` — the first build order

---

## 1. What this is

A reusable analytical method an actor can apply to any scene, monologue, speech,
conversation, interview, debate, or their own rehearsal text.

The governing question of the whole framework:

> **What is this person doing to that person with these words — and do they know what they're talking about?**

The framework is a thinking tool, not a compliance form. Completion never means
answering every question.

---

## 2. Philosophical sources — and how to use them

Three names inform the pedagogy. They do **not** play the same role, and collapsing them
into one list of "philosophies about speech" will produce confused content.

**Plato and Socrates supply method.** Dialectic, the examined claim, the question that
exposes an assumption, the difference between knowing and seeming to know. This is where
the dissection framework's *Questions* section and the future *Art of the Question*
block come from. Socratic method is a tool the learner picks up and uses.

**Mao supplies a case study.** Speech deployed as an instrument of state power — mass
persuasion, rectification, the extracted confession, art and literature bent to a
political program. This is material the learner *examines*, not a method they adopt. The
Yan'an Talks are useful to an actor precisely because they are a clear, articulate,
self-aware account of using language to produce a desired change in a listener at scale.

**The rule that keeps this honest:** the app must never present any of the three as an
authority the learner is expected to accept. Plato and Socrates are offered as tools.
Mao is offered as evidence.

**And a caution worth stating plainly.** If Mao is the app's only worked example of
speech-as-power, the app reads as political, and you have written a principle into your
own brief that forbids exactly that. Any curriculum block on manipulation and mass
persuasion should draw from a spread — state propaganda from more than one regime and
more than one century, advertising, courtroom summation, the pulpit, the campaign
speech, the recruiting pitch — so the learner comes away with a *method of detection*
rather than a political verdict. The four-speaker taxonomy below is the neutral spine
that makes this possible.

### The four speakers

Used throughout the curriculum. Never collapse the third and fourth.

| Type | Understanding | Emotion | Intent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grounded and expressive** | Substantial | Deepens meaning | Illuminate |
| **Grounded and restrained** | Substantial | Limited expression | Illuminate |
| **Ungrounded but emotional** | Weak or unexamined | Certainty | **Sincere** — they believe it |
| **Deliberate manipulator** | Often adequate | Strategic | Conceal, distort, prevent examination |

The third speaker is not lying. That distinction is the most useful thing in the
taxonomy, and it's the one an actor most needs in order to play either honestly.

|---|---|---|

### Quick Dissection — the six

1. What is happening?
2. What does the speaker want?
3. What is the listener resisting?
4. What is the speaker doing to change them?
5. Where does the exchange change?
6. What is different at the end?

## 4. The full framework

Ten sections. Each has a governing question. Question IDs are `section.slug` and are
**stable forever** — renaming an ID orphans a user's saved answer.

### 4.1 The Situation — `situation`

> **Why must these words be spoken now?**

- What has just happened?
- Where are these people?
- Why is this conversation happening now?
- What is at stake?
- What does each person stand to gain or lose?
- What happens if nothing changes?
- What pressures exist outside the immediate conversation?
- Is there a deadline, threat, opportunity, or approaching consequence?
- What does the audience know that the speakers may not?

### 4.2 The Speaker — `speaker`

> **Does the speaker's confidence match the strength of their knowledge?**

- Who is speaking?
- What do they want?
- What do they need from the listener, and why from *this* listener?
- What are they afraid of?
- What do they know, and how did they come to know it — direct experience, someone else's testimony, or assumption?
- How certain are they?
- What are they hiding from the listener?
- What might they be hiding from themselves?
- What do they want the listener to think of them?
- What would they never willingly admit?
- What changes for them during the exchange?

The learner should be able to separate: what the speaker **knows** · **believes** ·
**assumes** · **feels** · **claims** · **pretends to know** · **cannot yet admit**.

### 4.3 The Listener — `listener`

> **What must change inside the listener for the speaker to get what they want?**

- Who is being addressed?
- What do they currently believe?
- What do they know that the speaker does not?
- What do they want?
- What do they fear?
- What are they resisting?
- What would make them change?
- What relationship exists between them?
- Who has more power at the start — official, social, emotional, intellectual, or physical?
- What does the listener expect to hear?
- What do they refuse to hear?
- How do they react, even in silence?
- When does resistance weaken or strengthen?
- Who has more power at the end?

### 4.4 The Language — `language`

> **Why these words, in this order, for this listener?**

- What is the literal meaning?
- What is implied but unsaid?
- Which words carry the most weight?
- Which reveal the speaker's values?
- Where do they repeat themselves, and what does the repetition suggest?
- Where do they evade, or answer a different question from the one asked?
- Where does the language become more emotional, more precise, less precise?
- Is the speaker using evidence, testimony, threat, flattery, shame, humor, or authority?
- Which words are built to produce a reaction?
- Which terms are left undefined?
- Is abstraction being used to avoid something concrete?
- Are there certainty words — *always, never, everyone, obviously*?
- Where is observation, where is interpretation, where is assumption presented as fact?
- What important fact or question is missing?
- What changes if a particular word is stressed?
- Where does the thought change direction?

### 4.5 The Action — `action`

> **What is the speaker trying to do to the listener with these words?**

- What is the primary action?
- What response do they want?
- What tactic do they open with?
- When does it fail?
- What do they try next?
- Are words and action aligned?
- Is the apparent action concealing another?
- Does it become more direct or more indirect?
- What action finally succeeds or fails?

*Connects to the Action Library, §7.*

### 4.6 Emotion — `emotion`

> **What does the speaker feel?**
> **What are they trying to make the listener feel?**
> **What purpose does that feeling serve?**

- Is the emotion spontaneous, controlled, performed, or strategically produced?
- Does it clarify why the subject matters, or conceal weak understanding?
- Does it pressure the listener to stop examining the claim?
- Is the speaker feeling one thing and expressing another?
- Does the intensity match what is actually known?
- How does it change?
- What happens if the words are spoken with less intensity?
- What happens if the emotion stays but the action changes?

### 4.7 Knowledge and Reasoning — `knowledge`

> **How does the speaker know what they are asking the listener to believe?**

- Which statements are observable facts? Personal experience? Testimony? Interpretation? Opinion? Assumption about another person?
- What evidence is offered, and is it relevant?
- Could the same evidence support a different conclusion?
- What is omitted?
- Does the speaker know what they claim to know?
- Does confidence exceed understanding?
- Does emotional force exceed evidence?
- What requires investigation?
- What would cause the speaker to revise?
- Is the listener persuaded because the claim is strong or because the delivery is?

### 4.8 The Questions — `questions`

> **What has not yet been asked?**

- What questions are asked?
- Which are answered directly, which avoided?
- Which carry hidden assumptions?
- Is a question seeking understanding or disguising an accusation?
- What should the listener ask?
- What would expose the weakest assumption?
- What question could deepen understanding rather than escalate conflict?
- What is the speaker afraid to answer?
- What should the speaker ask themselves?
- Would a better question change the exchange?

### 4.9 The Turning Point — `turning`

> **When does this become a different conversation?**

- When does new information appear?
- When does the listener begin to change?
- When does resistance increase?
- Which word, silence, realization, or reaction causes the shift?
- Does the speaker change tactics?
- Does power shift?
- Does someone finally say what the conversation was actually about?
- What can no longer be denied?
- Is there more than one turning point?

### 4.10 The Result — `result`

> **What did these words change?**

- Did the speaker get what they wanted?
- Did the listener understand? Did they agree?
- Were they persuaded, pressured, confused, or overwhelmed?
- Did the relationship change?
- Did power shift?
- Was something revealed?
- Was the speech truthful? Effective? Ethical? Emotionally powerful?
- Did it deepen understanding?
- Can it be effective without being truthful?
- Can it be truthful without being effective?
- What remains unresolved?

## 5. The Art of the Question

A future curriculum block. Core principle:

> The quality of the questions you ask shapes the quality of the understanding you can reach.

**Define it**

- What do you mean?
- How are you using that word?

**Ground it**

- How do you know?
- What did you observe?
- What came from elsewhere?

**Examine it**

- What are you assuming?
- Could the same facts support another explanation?

**Test it**

- Is this always true?
- What would change your mind?

**Weigh it**

- Why does this matter?
- Who is affected?
- What if we're wrong?

**Turn it inward**

- Why do I believe this?
- Where did I get it?
- Am I defending the idea or my identity?
- What am I afraid I might find?

The dissection data model must be able to hold user-authored questions (§8) so this block
has somewhere to land.

## 6. Watch · Listen · Read · Perform

| Mode | Reveals |
|---|---|
| **Watch** | Body, gesture, gaze, relationship, status, visible reaction, apparent control |
| **Listen** | Pace, pause, pitch, rhythm, emphasis, breath, hesitation, interruption, pressure |
| **Read** | Claims, evidence, word choice, repetition, evasion, assumption, contradiction, structure |
| **Perform** | Action, intention, responsiveness, how choices land on the listener |

Eventual sequence: watch without analysis → record first impression → listen without the
image → read and mark → dissect → watch again → choose actions → perform variants →
observe effect → reflect and revise.

**Media rule, absolute:** never fabricate a clip, never show a Watch or Listen control
without working approved media behind it, never introduce external hosting. Text-first is
the honest foundation. Architect so media can be added later without a rewrite.

## 7. The Action Library

> **What are you doing to the other person through these words?**

The teaching distinction: **emotion is what the speaker feels; action is what the speaker
is doing to the listener.** An actor who plays sadness gives a performance about
themselves. An actor who plays *to shame* gives a performance about someone else.

### Placement — recommendation

It belongs in **TEXT BOOK**, as a sixth card beside IPA, Native Idioms, Texts & Speeches,
Your Instrument, and The Vowel Map. It's a reference work; that's the reference shelf. No
new top-level navigation.

Name, in the house style of the existing cards:

| Option | Card subtitle | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Playable Actions** *(recommended)* | "What you're doing to the other person." | Matches *Your Instrument* / *The Vowel Map* — plain noun phrase, actor's vocabulary, unmistakably practical |
| The Action Library | "Verbs to play, and what they do to a listener." | Clear, slightly more institutional |
| What You're Doing | "The verb underneath the line." | Most conversational, least searchable |

### Categories and verbs

**To Connect** — comfort · reassure · welcome · encourage · include · reconcile · confide · befriend
**To Persuade** — convince · inspire · recruit · challenge · reason with · appeal to · motivate · warn
**To Discover** — question · investigate · test · clarify · confront · expose · draw out · understand
**To Protect** — defend · deflect · conceal · deny · justify · excuse · distance · disarm
**To Control** — command · pressure · intimidate · shame · provoke · silence · corner · manipulate
**To Change the Relationship** — seduce · impress · dismiss · forgive · punish · humble · elevate · alienate
**To Reveal** — confess · admit · explain · teach · demonstrate · name · declare · testify

Fifty-six verbs, seven categories. **Do not ship fifty-six shallow entries.** A dozen
fully written ones beats the full set half-done — and the half-done ones will never get
finished once the category looks populated.

### Entry schema

```js
{
  id: 'reassure',
  category: 'connect',
  verb: 'Reassure',
  objective: '...',      // the change you want in the listener
  resistance: '...',     // what stands in the way
  coaching: '...',       // the note that stops them playing an emotion
  contrast: { verb: 'dismiss', why: '...' },
  practiceLine: '...',
  vocal: '...',          // optional
  example: '...',        // optional
}
```

### Worked example — the standard to match

**To Reassure**

*Objective:* Help the listener believe they are safe, or that the situation can be
managed.

*Likely resistance:* Fear, distrust, uncertainty.

*Coaching:* Do not "act calm." Put your attention on changing the listener's expectation
of danger. Calm is what it may look like from outside; it is not what you are doing.

*Contrast:* **To dismiss** can also sound calm — but reassurance takes the listener's fear
seriously, and dismissal treats it as unimportant. Same tone, opposite action.

### Suggested first twelve

One from each category, plus the pairs that teach the sharpest contrast:
`reassure` / `dismiss` · `confront` / `deflect` · `confess` / `conceal` ·
`command` / `appeal to` · `challenge` / `shame` · `draw out` / `corner`.

Every pair can sound identical and does something opposite. That's the lesson.

## 8. Data model

Analyses are large, unbounded, and user-authored — **IndexedDB**, alongside projects and
recordings. Never localStorage.

```js
// store: 'dissections', keyPath 'id', index on 'targetKey'
{
  id: 'diss-<uuid>',
  schemaVersion: 1,

  targetKey: 'project:proj-123',      // or 'library:IBSEN-001'
  targetType: 'project' | 'library',
  targetId:   'proj-123',
  targetLabel: 'Nora — The Secret Loan',   // denormalized, for listing without a join

  materialType: 'monologue' | 'scene' | 'speech' | 'conversation' | 'interview' | 'debate',
  mode: 'quick' | 'guided' | 'full',
  level: 'beginner' | 'intermediate' | 'advanced',

  createdAt, updatedAt,

  answers: {
    // key is the stable question id — NEVER rename one
    'situation.stakes': {
      value: 'She could lose the marriage and the children.',
      status: 'answered' | 'unknown' | 'na',   // "I don't know yet" / "Not relevant"
      updatedAt,
    },
  },

  actions: [
    { verb: 'reassure', scope: 'whole', passageRef: null, note: '' },
  ],

  // reserved — declared now so later features don't need a migration
  annotations: [],     // line/passage level
  speakers: [],        // multi-speaker scenes
  interpretations: [], // alternative readings
  userQuestions: [],   // The Art of the Question
  history: [],         // earlier vs revised answers
}
```

Rules:

- `status: 'unknown'` and `status: 'na'` are **first-class answers**, not empty states.
  A dissection where the actor has honestly marked six things "I don't know yet" is a
  more useful document than one padded with guesses.
- Completion is never "all questions answered." Show coverage, not a score.
- Deleting an analysis is separate from deleting the project. Confirm before either.
- Migrations versioned, narrow, backward-compatible. Never silent.
- Every stored string is untrusted on read — it round-trips through IndexedDB and gets
  rendered as HTML. `esc()` without exception.

## 9. Progression

| Level | Adds |
|---|---|
| **Beginner** | What's happening · what the speaker wants · what the listener resists · what action is played · what changes |
| **Intermediate** | Knows vs assumes · what's implied · what emotion they're producing · where tactics change · the turning point |
| **Advanced** | How the knowledge was acquired · confidence vs evidence · assumptions hidden inside questions · what's omitted · does emotion clarify or conceal · was it persuasive, truthful, ethical |

Store `level` per dissection. Don't gate — an advanced actor doing quick work should be
able to drop to Beginner without being told they've regressed.

## 10. Material-type adaptations

**Monologue**

- Who is addressed?
- Why do they keep going?
- What responses are observed or imagined?
- What is discovered *while* speaking?

**Scene**

- What does each want?
- What does each know?
- Where do they misunderstand each other?
- How does one action change the other?
- When does status shift?

**Public speech**

- Who is the audience?
- What do they already believe?
- What are they asked to believe or do?
- What evidence and appeals are used?
- Which objections are ignored?

**Debate**

- What is the central question?
- Are terms defined?
- Which claims are supported?
- Which questions are avoided?
- Is either side misrepresenting the other?
- Does anyone revise a position?

**Personal conversation**

- What does each need?
- What does each assume?
- What is said indirectly?
- Where does defensiveness start?
- What question could deepen understanding?
- What is left unsaid?

## 11. UX principles

- A thinking tool, not a worksheet. No completion percentage that shames.
- "I don't know yet" is valuable and must be one tap.
- Progressive disclosure. Never a wall of fifty inputs.
- Real `<label>` elements. Placeholders are not labels.
- Autosave, with a status the user can actually see. Never lose an unfinished thought.
- Enter, leave, return — always.
- Plain language over academic register wherever both work.
- Analysis must lead back to speaking. A dissection that ends in a saved document and not
  a spoken line has failed at the thing the app is for.

## 13. Open questions for the owner

1. **Does the threshold award XP?** And does *Learn the Craft* become a sixth track in
   LEARN beside the five dialect tracks, or live outside that system? This decides whether
   the feature reads as native or bolted on.
2. **Has the framework been run by hand, once, end to end?** Before any UI: do a full
   Guided pass on a real piece with a pen. If it's exhausting to complete once, no
   interface fixes that — and it's far cheaper to learn now.
3. **Which integration point first** — a My Texts project, or a Texts & Speeches library
   piece? Projects are the actor's real work and the natural home; library pieces are
   easier because the text is fixed and known.
4. **US or British spelling** for user-facing copy. The repo currently mixes.
