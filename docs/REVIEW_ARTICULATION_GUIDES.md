# Review packet: articulation guides

**62 items. Reviewer needed: a qualified voice professional or speech-language
pathologist.**

These tell a learner what to do with their body, which makes them
professional-tier under the project's own review rule. They are LIVE in the app
right now, each badged "Prepared draft, awaiting review by a qualified voice
professional", alongside the hand-drawn diagram for that sound.

## What to check

1. **Is the articulatory description accurate?** Place, manner, voicing, tongue
   height and backness, lip posture.
2. **Is any instruction unsafe or likely to cause strain?** Nothing here should
   ask for force, pushing or held tension.
3. **Is the contrast pair the right one**, and is the stated difference the real
   one?
4. **Does the watch-out line describe accent variation fairly?** These
   deliberately say "that is normal" rather than "do not do that" for real
   accent features (terminal devoicing, dark /l/, h-dropping, /ŋg/,
   monophthongal FACE, northern STRUT, cot-caught merger).

## How to record a verdict

Per item, not in a batch, and with your name against it. An unnamed or
batch approval does not count under the project's review rule.

## /p/  p

*Example words: pen, happy, cup*

**Summary.** Both lips closed, pressure behind them, then released. No voice.

**Steps.**
1. Close both lips and stop the air completely.
2. Let a little pressure build behind them.
3. Release. In English, at the start of a stressed syllable /p/ comes with a puff of air. Hold a hand in front of your mouth on "pie" and you should feel it.

**Against /b/.** /b/ is the same closure and release with the voice on, and without the puff. Pie and buy differ by nothing else.

**Watch out.** At the end of a word ("cap") the release can be very small or absent. That is normal English, not a mistake.

## /t/  t

*Example words: tea, better, cat*

**Summary.** Tongue tip seals against the ridge behind your top teeth, then releases. No voice.

**Steps.**
1. Press the tip of your tongue against the ridge just behind your top teeth, sealing the air in.
2. Let pressure build behind the seal.
3. Release. At the start of a stressed syllable it carries a puff of air. Hold a hand in front of your mouth on "tea" and you should feel it.

**Against /d/.** /d/ is the same seal and release with the voice on, and without the puff.

**Watch out.** Between vowels many accents tap it, and before a consonant or at the end of a word many replace it with a glottal stop. Both are ways of saying /t/, not different consonants.

## /k/  k

*Example words: cat, key, school*

**Summary.** The back of the tongue lifts to the soft palate, seals the air in, then lets it go. No voice.

**Steps.**
1. Lift the back of your tongue until it seals against the soft palate, at the very back of the roof of your mouth.
2. Let pressure build behind the seal. Nothing escapes.
3. Release. At the start of a stressed syllable the release carries a puff of air. Hold a hand in front of your mouth on "cat" and you should feel it.

**Against /g/.** /g/ is the same seal and release with the voice on, and without the puff. Coat and goat differ by nothing else.

**Watch out.** The contact is further back than most people expect. If you feel it near your teeth you have made /t/.

## /ʔ/  glottal stop

*Example words: better, water, got*

**Summary.** Nothing happens in the mouth. The vocal folds themselves close, cut the sound off, and release.

**Steps.**
1. Leave your tongue and lips wherever the surrounding sounds put them. This sound has no mouth position of its own.
2. Close your vocal folds so the sound stops completely, the way they close before a cough.
3. Release them.

**Against /t/.** This is a way of saying /t/, not a separate consonant. Better as [beʔə] and [betə] is the same word either way.

**Watch out.** Use varies by speaker, by setting and by where it falls in the word, and careful speech tends to keep [t]. It is a choice, not a rule to apply everywhere.

## /m/  m

*Example words: man, summer, lamb*

**Summary.** Lips closed, soft palate dropped, the voice going out through your nose.

**Steps.**
1. Close both lips gently.
2. Let the soft palate drop so the voice goes up and out through your nose.
3. Voice on. Pinch your nose while holding the sound and it should stop dead.

**Against /b/.** /b/ has the same lip closure, but the soft palate stays raised so pressure builds and bursts. /m/ never builds pressure: the air has somewhere to go.

**Watch out.** You can hold this as long as your breath lasts, which is why it is used for humming and warm-ups.

## /ŋ/  eng

*Example words: sing, long, thanks*

**Summary.** The back of the tongue seals against the soft palate and the voice comes out through your nose instead.

**Steps.**
1. Lift the back of your tongue to the soft palate, the same seal you make for /k/.
2. Keep that seal and let the soft palate drop, opening the way up into your nose.
3. Voice on throughout. Pinch your nose while holding the sound and it should stop dead. That is the test that the air is going where it should.

**Against /n/.** /n/ sends the voice out through the nose in exactly the same way, but seals at the ridge behind your teeth instead of at the back. Sin and sing differ only in where the tongue blocks.

**Watch out.** In most English accents "sing" ends here, with no /g/ after it. Some accents do add one. That is a feature of those accents, not a fault.

## /f/  f

*Example words: fat, coffee, rough*

**Summary.** Top teeth resting on the bottom lip, air pushing through, no voice.

**Steps.**
1. Rest your top teeth lightly on your bottom lip. Lightly: this is a contact, not a bite.
2. Push air out so it hisses between teeth and lip.
3. Keep the voice off. A finger on your throat should feel nothing.

**Against /v/.** /v/ is the same mouth with the voice on. Alternate them on one breath: ffff-vvvv. Only the buzz changes.

**Watch out.** If it sounds like /p/, the lips have met each other instead of teeth meeting lip.

## /θ/  theta

*Example words: think, author, bath*

**Summary.** Tongue tip against or between the teeth, air hissing through, no voice.

## /s/  s

*Example words: see, missing, bus*

**Summary.** Tongue tip close to the ridge behind your top teeth, air hissing through a narrow channel, no voice.

## /ʃ/  esh

*Example words: ship, sure, station*

**Summary.** The tongue drawn back from the /s/ position, the channel wider, lips usually rounded. No voice.

**Steps.**

## /h/  h

*Example words: hat, ahead, who*

**Summary.** No mouth position of its own: breath through an open throat while the mouth is already shaped for the vowel that follows.

**Steps.**
1. Set your mouth for the vowel that comes next, and do not move it.
2. Push a breath through the open throat before the voice starts.
3. Let the voice come in as the vowel begins.

**Against /ʔ/.** The glottal stop closes the vocal folds. /h/ leaves them open and lets breath through. They are opposites made in the same place.

**Watch out.** Dropping it at the start of words is a well-known feature of many accents. It is a feature rather than a fault, but it is accent-specific: check which accent you are in.

## /tʃ/  ch

*Example words: chin, teacher, match*

**Summary.** A closure released slowly into a hiss, so a stop and a fricative run together as one sound.

**Steps.**
1. Seal the tongue against the ridge as for /t/, but slightly further back.
2. Release slowly instead of sharply, so the air escapes as a hiss.
3. Voice off throughout. The stop and the hiss are one sound, not two.

**Against /dʒ/.** The same closure and slow release with the voice on. Chin and gin differ by nothing else.

**Watch out.** Releasing too fast gives a plain /t/. Not closing first gives a plain /ʃ/. The sound needs both halves.

## /l/  l

*Example words: leg, yellow, feel*

**Summary.** Tongue tip on the ridge behind your top teeth, the sides dropped, voice flowing out around it.

**Steps.**
1. Touch the tip of your tongue to the ridge behind your top teeth and keep it there.
2. Drop the sides of your tongue so the voice escapes around them. That side channel is what makes this /l/ and not /d/.
3. Voice on. You can hold the sound for as long as your breath lasts.

**Against /r/.** For /r/ the tongue never touches anything. If the tip makes contact you have said /l/. Say "led" and "red" slowly and feel where the difference is.

**Watch out.** Before a consonant and at the end of a word the back of the tongue usually humps up and the sound darkens. Leaf and feel use noticeably different tongue shapes.

## /r/  r

*Example words: red, sorry, try*

**Summary.** The tongue raised toward the ridge but never touching it, with the voice flowing through the gap.

**Steps.**
1. Raise the tip of your tongue toward the ridge behind your top teeth and stop before it touches. Or bunch the body of the tongue upward instead. Both are used by native speakers.
2. Keep the sides of your tongue against the upper teeth so the air runs down the centre.
3. Voice on. Many speakers round the lips slightly as well.

**Against /l/.** /l/ touches the ridge; /r/ does not. Red and led differ by that contact alone.

**Watch out.** Whether it is pronounced at the end of a word depends entirely on the accent. Broad transcription writes /r/, while the sound most speakers actually make is [ɹ].

## /w/  w

*Example words: wet, away, one*

**Summary.** Lips rounded and the back of the tongue raised at the same time, then moving straight into the next vowel.

**Steps.**
1. Round your lips tightly, as if for a close back vowel.
2. At the same time raise the back of your tongue toward the soft palate.
3. Voice on, and move immediately into the vowel. This sound only exists while it is moving.

**Against /uː/.** The GOOSE vowel holds the same shape still. /w/ never holds: it is that shape in motion toward something else.

**Watch out.** Two things happen at once here, lips and tongue back. Doing only the lips gives a weak, half-made sound.

## /ɪ/  KIT vowel

*Example words: kit, bid, ship*

**Summary.** Tongue front high but relaxed, jaw slightly open, lips loose.

**Steps.**
1. Start from the FLEECE position, tongue front and high.
2. Let it drop and relax a little, and let the jaw open slightly.
3. Keep it short and loose. The muscles should feel slack, not held.

**Against /iː/.** FLEECE is higher, tenser and longer. Ship and sheep differ by tongue height and tension, not by length alone.

**Watch out.** Aiming for a short /iː/ gives the wrong vowel. The looseness is the point of it.

## /æ/  TRAP vowel

*Example words: trap, cat, hand*

**Summary.** Jaw well open, tongue low and forward, lips relaxed.

**Steps.**
1. Drop your jaw until the mouth is clearly open, further than for the DRESS vowel.
2. Keep the body of your tongue forward and low, down behind your bottom teeth.
3. Leave the lips relaxed. Rounding them pulls the sound toward a different vowel entirely.

**Against /e/.** The DRESS vowel sits higher with a narrower jaw. Say "bed" then "bad" and feel the jaw drop on the second.

**Watch out.** How open and how long this vowel is varies a great deal between accents. Match the accent you are working in rather than aiming for one universal version.

## /ʌ/  STRUT vowel

*Example words: but, cup, love*

**Summary.** Jaw fairly open, tongue low and slightly behind centre, lips relaxed.

**Steps.**
1. Drop the jaw to roughly half open.
2. Keep the tongue low, a little behind centre, and completely relaxed.
3. Lips neutral. No rounding at all.

**Against /ɒ/.** LOT is rounded and further back. Cup and cop.

**Watch out.** This vowel does not exist in many accents of northern England, where the FOOT vowel is used instead. That is a genuine accent difference, not an error.

## /ɜː/  NURSE vowel

*Example words: nurse, stir, learn*

**Summary.** Tongue flat in the middle of the mouth, jaw slightly open, lips neutral, held long.

**Steps.**
1. Let the tongue sit centrally, neither forward nor back, at about mid height.
2. Open the jaw a little.
3. Keep the lips neutral and hold the position. Nothing moves.
