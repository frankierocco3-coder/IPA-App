# Building a Character — the Bogosian method

Design proposal. Source: Eric Bogosian, "Building Character: My Method for
Creating the Solos", pages 303 to 315, the afterword to *Wake Up and Smell the
Coffee*.

Read from the owner's own scans. **The passages the owner bracketed by hand are
treated as the spine of the module**; every one of them is accounted for below.

## The collision to resolve first

**Bogosian's method is recording-first.** It begins with an actor named David
Warrilow telling him to buy a tape recorder, record his voice and listen back,
because that would make him his own teacher. Everything after that is improvise
aloud, listen, transcribe, improvise again.

**Speechcraft has `CAPABILITIES.learnerSpeaking = false`, frozen.** The app does
not record and deliberately never asks a learner to make a sound.

These do not have to fight. Bogosian used a cheap cassette recorder, not a
theatre. **The recording happens on the learner's own phone, outside the app.**
Speechcraft is the notebook, not the microphone: it holds the prompt bank, the
transcriptions, the catalogue of people found, and the questions asked later in
rehearsal.

The app already tells learners to do things it cannot observe, and says so
plainly. This is the same, at larger scale, and the copy must be honest that the
work happens off the page.

## The method, extracted

### How it started

Improvising aloud into a recorder with no text and no plan. Doing a voice for
the sake of doing it. Listening back and discovering that a monologue had
happened without being intended, and that someone had been waiting to speak.

Then cataloguing them. Bogosian sorted his improvs into twelve male archetypes,
from a street punk to a deer hunter to a small boy. The decisive point is the
direction of the search: **he was not looking outward for characters, he was
looking inward.**

### The working process

The part the owner bracketed on page 308, in order:

1. A recorder and an empty room. Isolated, nobody able to overhear, and enough
   space to move around in.
2. Being alone is the condition, not a convenience. Self-consciousness kills the
   work.
3. Note the date and the piece. Start.
4. Review the recording and find the parts you like **the sound of**.
5. Transcribe those. Keep the transcriptions and go back to them later.
6. Select from them, learn those fragments, and use them as the launch pad for
   the next improv.
7. Repeat.

A finished monologue of roughly three minutes comes out of hours of improvising.

Why speaking rather than writing: less editorialising, looser language, and an
arc that stays unpredictable in the way real talk is. People wander, get
interrupted, follow side thoughts, and react to each other.

### Three ways into a character

- **An overheard fragment.** He collects scraps of real speech and starts by
  repeating one like a mantra until it summons an attitude. A single key word can
  carry a whole person, as "sir" does for the airline counter clerk in *Wake Up*.
- **A physical detail.** The way a junkie nods into the flame while lighting a
  cigarette. How someone holds a bottle. How an old man crosses a room.
- **A vocal stance, which he calls the most powerful of the three.** Not
  mimicry, which he considers hollow, but letting a vocal posture shape the
  improvisation from inside. His test case is reciting the Gettysburg Address in
  a Minnie Mouse voice.

And a fourth, bracketed on page 312: **natural performance situations.**
Behaviour in front of an audience is always performance, whoever the audience
is, so a teacher, a preacher, a gym instructor and a man shouting in the street
are all ready-made launch points.

### The rule while improvising

Bracketed on pages 309 and 310. Do not aim at anything. Do not try for funny,
do not try for poignant. Become the person, enter the situation, and find out
what happens. Play and cut loose and let the character speak. It is not the
moment to worry about how the finished thing will sound, or whether the
character is articulate.

### What a character is

Bracketed on page 314. Characters are not fixed objects. They are closer to
clouds of behaviour, attitude and statement, and one shades into the next. They
are contrivances with no external reality to be checked against, so the good one
is simply the one with the most force. He describes experimenting until he gets
the character who, in his phrase, sits up and lives.

### The theory underneath

From pages 306 and 307, and worth keeping because it justifies the method:

- Theatre is character. Everything else is decoration.
- We are all already performing. A doctor behaves like a doctor because that is
  how doctors have been shown to behave. Behaviour is learned from outside, not
  innate.
- You do not need to have lived it. Playing a soldier, Bogosian draws on
  remembered performances of soldiers as much as on his own life, and his
  subconscious does not distinguish. Picasso is invoked: a lie that tells the
  truth.
- Everyone runs a small theatre inside their head, populated by conceptual
  versions of real people. The mother in your mind is as much your idea of her
  as she is herself.
- The job is to put that conceptual person onstage so that an audience
  recognises her. If they do not recognise it, the play does not work. Theatre
  is consensus.

### Shaping it afterwards

- Transcribe, memorise, rehearse, discuss with a director, workshop it, run it,
  tour it. Each performance is a check on logic, tone, humour and rhythm.
- Bracketed on page 311: the clearer and more consistent the piece becomes, the
  easier it is to memorise and perform. Clarity and memorability are the same
  problem.
- Bracketed on page 313: keep the character active, standing, moving, engaged.
  Avoid mime and costume, which distract. He wants, in his phrase, "the essence
  of the character, not the hat".
- The no-language test: imagine an audience that does not speak the character's
  language. Would they still watch?
- Play against expectation. A character who says the opposite of what is
  expected is more alive than one delivering the author's opinion.
- Even the worst person in the piece has his own side of the story.

### The questions that come later

Bracketed on page 315. In rehearsal he returns to plain questions the early
improvising skipped: what was this person doing ten minutes ago, what are they
wearing and carrying, how old are they and what does that do to the voice and
the posture, are we indoors or out, is it warm or cold. He notes these are
ordinary acting-class questions, and that their value is in jogging the
imagination rather than in the answers.

### The final test

Bracketed on page 315, and the right closing line for the module: he puts on
stage what he would most want to see if he were sitting in the audience.

## Proposed structure

This is a module's worth of material on its own, and it comes **before**
tempo-rhythm: first you find the person, then you work out how they move through
time. Which means the Acting course grows by two modules, not one.

| # | Module | Lessons |
|---|---|---:|
| 1 | The Actor's Work | 8 |
| 2 | Investigating the Text | 8 |
| 3 | Listening and Responding | 6 |
| 4 | **Building a Character** | **7** |
| 5 | **Tempo-Rhythm** | **6** |
| 6 | Preparing the Performance | 6 |

Splitting them also fixes a problem in the earlier proposal, where a module
called Building a Character was six-sevenths tempo-rhythm.

### Module 4 · Building a Character

| # | Lesson | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | The Four Lists | Existing. The written inventories, as the entry point |
| 4.2 | Looking Inside, Not Out | The cast already in you; cataloguing them; characters as clouds rather than fixed objects |
| 4.3 | The Empty Room | Conditions for improvising: alone, unobserved, room to move, recorder running, self-consciousness as the enemy |
| 4.4 | Three Ways In | Overheard fragment, physical detail, vocal stance. Plus natural performance situations |
| 4.5 | Do Not Aim | The improvising rule: not funny, not poignant, just become and see |
| 4.6 | Listen, Transcribe, Go Again | The loop, and three minutes out of hours. Clarity and memorability as one problem |
| 4.7 | The Essence, Not the Hat | Active characters, the no-language test, playing against expectation, the jerk's side of the story |

The rehearsal questions from page 315 belong at the end of 4.6, where the
learner has something written down to interrogate.

### Module 5 · Tempo-Rhythm

Unchanged from the existing draft: Two Dials Not One, Rhythm Reaches Feeling,
Two Rhythms at Once, Harmony Not Unison, Notes and Rests, The Two Missing
Speeds. Copy already written in `docs/BUILD_TEMPO_RHYTHM_COPY.md`.

One connection worth making explicit in 5.1: Bogosian, arriving from a completely
different direction, ends up talking about arranging words into something close
to a rhythm and calling it the music of the words. Two practitioners, one
conclusion, and it is worth telling a learner that.

## Tools this implies

**A character catalogue.** The people you have found, each with the fragment,
detail or vocal stance that started them. Stored per user rather than per
project, because a character outlives a script.

**A transcription store.** Bogosian keeps his transcriptions and returns to them.
This is the notebook function, and it is the piece the app can genuinely own.

**A prompt bank.** Overheard fragments the learner collects, ready to launch an
improvisation. Text entry, no audio.

All three are text and storage only. No recording, no scoring, no audio.

## Open questions

1. **Is the off-app recording acceptable?** The method needs a phone and a room.
   Speechcraft would be telling learners to do something it cannot see. Honest,
   but new for this app at this scale.
2. **Two modules or one?** Splitting gives Building a Character its own identity.
   Keeping them together gives one very large module.
3. **Does the character catalogue belong in Studio** beside projects, or in its
   own place? It is the first thing in the app that belongs to the actor rather
   than to a script.
