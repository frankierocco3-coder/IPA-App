# Modules 4 and 5 — third pass at the copy

The first draft read literary, around nineteen words a sentence. The simplified
pass swung hard the other way, around eight, and some of it landed choppy. This
pass sits between them: plain words, an even walking pace, one idea per
sentence, but sentences that breathe. Same rules as always: no practitioner
named, no em dashes, no contractions.

# Module 4 · Building a Character

## 4.1 · Looking Inside, Not Out

**Objective.** Start from the people already in you.

**Orientation.** Observation is usually taught as looking outward. This module
looks the other way.

**Body.**

> You are already carrying a cast of people. Improvise out loud with no plan,
> doing a voice just for the fun of it, then listen back to the recording. You
> will often find a monologue you never meant to make, spoken by someone who
> seems to have been waiting for the chance.

> This works because you have been collecting people your whole life. Some of
> them you met. Others you watched in films or heard across a room. The part of
> you that produces behavior draws on all of them equally, and it does not
> bother sorting the real ones from the borrowed.

> So do not treat a character as an object you finish. Treat it as a person you
> are trying to get breathing. Keep experimenting until one of them stands up
> and starts talking on its own.

**Reflection.** Who has spoken up in you before, unprompted? A voice you can
fall into without preparing it.

---

## 4.2 · The Empty Room

**Objective.** Set up a space where you can improvise without watching yourself.

**Orientation.** This part does not take talent. It takes a room where nobody
can hear you.

**Body.**

> Keep the setup plain. You need a recorder, a room where you are truly alone,
> and enough space to move around. Note the date, hit record, and begin.

> The privacy is not a comfort, it is the working condition. The moment any
> part of you starts managing how this sounds to someone else, you have stopped
> finding and started editing, and the thing you were about to discover will
> not show up.

> Record everything and keep all of it. This way of working was born on
> cassette tape, when every reel cost money and had to be stored somewhere, so
> people were forced to judge the work while they were still making it. That
> limit is gone. Your phone will hold more hours than you will ever fill, which
> means the judging can wait until later, where it belongs.

> Speechcraft cannot do this part for you. The app does not record and does not
> listen. Use your own phone, a closed door, and half an hour nobody needs you
> for. Bring back whatever you decide is worth keeping.

**Reflection.** Where and when could you do this without being overheard? Name
the room and the hour.

---

## 4.3 · Three Ways In

**Objective.** Start an improvisation from a fragment, a physical detail or a
vocal stance.

**Orientation.** You do not need a whole character to begin. You need one small
piece of one.

**Body.**

> The first way in is an overheard fragment. Collect scraps of real speech as
> you move through your week, then start an improv by repeating one until it
> wakes up an attitude. A single word can carry an entire person. Repeat “sir”
> enough times and an officious clerk will assemble himself around it.

> The second way in is a physical detail. The way someone lights a cigarette,
> or holds a bottle, or crosses a room. Work one honest gesture and the rest of
> the body tends to follow it in.

> The third way in is the strongest: a vocal stance. This is not an impression.
> It means letting a way of speaking shape the improv from the inside. Take
> something you know by heart, say it in a voice nothing like your own, and
> listen to what happens to the words.

> There is a fourth door worth knowing. Some people are already performing:
> a teacher in front of a class, a preacher, a gym instructor, a man shouting
> at traffic. Situations like that are ready-made starting points.

**Reflection.** Write down three fragments of speech you overheard this week.
Which one has a person standing behind it?

---

## 4.4 · Do Not Aim

**Objective.** Improvise without steering toward a result.

**Orientation.** This work fails one way more than any other: trying to make it
good while it is still happening.

**Body.**

> While you improvise, aim at nothing. Not funny, not moving, not clever.
> Become the person, get into the situation, and find out what happens. Letting
> the character talk is the entire job.

> The finished piece is not your problem yet. It does not matter whether the
> character rambles, or repeats, or makes no sense. Judging while you create
> shuts the creating down, and creating is the only thing this stage is for.

> Expect most of it to be waste, and make the waste on purpose: throw
> everything at the wall and see what sticks. Three usable minutes come out of
> hours of improvising. That ratio is the method working exactly as designed,
> and knowing it in advance is what carries you through the first bad stretch.

**Reflection.** Play back something you improvised. Where can you hear yourself
trying to be good?

---

## 4.5 · Listen, Transcribe, Go Again

**Objective.** Turn raw improvisation into material through a loop you repeat.

**Orientation.** You are not making a piece yet. You are producing dirt to sift.

**Body.**

> Listen back and pull out the parts you like the sound of. Not the smartest
> ideas, the parts that sound like a person. This is sifting, and the ratio is
> supposed to be poor: you gather a lot of dirt to find a little gold.

> Write the good parts down and keep them, even the ones you cannot use yet. A
> scrap that reads as nothing in March can start a whole person in September.

> Then clean the gold you found. Work the fragment over, learn it by heart, and
> use it to launch the next improvisation. Pocket what you keep and go back for
> the next batch of dirt. Each pass through the loop leaves the pile a little
> richer than the last.

> Once something is on paper, ask it the plain questions the improvising
> skipped over. What was this person doing ten minutes ago? What are they
> wearing and carrying? How old are they, and what does that age do to the
> voice and the posture? The answers matter less than what they wake up.

**Reflection.** Read your oldest transcription again. Is there someone in it
you did not notice at the time?

---

## 4.6 · The Person, Not the Props

**Objective.** Keep the character active and clear without decorating them.

**Orientation.** A costume and a mimed steering wheel are two ways of hiding a
character who is not there.

**Body.**

> Keep the character active: standing, moving, engaged with something. Skip the
> mime and skip the costume. Both of them invite the audience to grade your
> imitation instead of watching the person, and the person is the only thing on
> offer.

> Here is a test that works. Imagine playing this for an audience that does not
> speak the language. Would they still want to watch? If the answer depends on
> the words being understood, the character is not yet doing enough on their
> own.

> Two habits worth keeping. Let the character say the opposite of what people
> expect, because a person who surprises is more alive than one who delivers
> your opinions for you. And give the worst person in the piece their own side
> of the story. Then apply the final test to all of it: put on stage what you
> would most want to see if you were the one sitting in the audience.

**Reflection.** Watch your character with the sound off in your head. Is there
anything to see?

# Module 5 · Tempo-Rhythm

## 5.1 · Two Dials, Not One

**Objective.** Separate tempo from rhythm, and know which one is wrong.

**Orientation.** Most actors use one word for two different things. Splitting
them gives you two controls where you had one.

**Body.**

> Tempo is how fast you are going. Rhythm is the pattern of long and short
> inside that speed. The two move independently, which is why you can be fast
> and smooth, fast and jagged, slow and steady, or slow and broken.

> Telling them apart matters because the repairs are different. If the tempo is
> wrong, you change the speed. If the rhythm is wrong, changing the speed just
> carries the same bad pattern along at a new rate. That is why the note to
> pick up the pace so often fixes nothing: the scene was dragging in its
> rhythm, not its tempo.

> Three traps are worth knowing by name. Some actors are rhythmic in the body
> and arhythmic in speech, or the other way round. Some carry one rhythm into
> every role, so they are always recognizably themselves. And some deliver the
> metre perfectly with nothing underneath it.

**Reflection.** Take a speech you know well. Is the problem its speed, or the
pattern inside the speed?

---

## 5.2 · Rhythm Reaches Feeling

**Objective.** Use tempo-rhythm as a way into feeling.

**Orientation.** Feeling cannot be ordered around. Speed can. That gap is the
whole usefulness of this idea.

**Body.**

> There are three doors into an actor. The text reaches the mind. The objective
> reaches the will. Tempo-rhythm reaches the feelings, and the feelings answer
> to nothing else. You cannot instruct yourself to dread something, but you can
> set the rhythm of dread and let the feeling walk in behind it.

> It does not work in a vacuum. A rhythm with nothing behind it is just
> drumming, and it produces nothing that outlasts the exercise. Give the rhythm
> a circumstance to sit on and the two start feeding each other: the situation
> shapes the rhythm, and the rhythm shows you more of the situation than you
> had imagined.

> Remember that speed alone never describes a moment. A march, a stroll and a
> funeral procession can all move at the same tempo and share nothing else.
> When a rhythm feels arbitrary, what is missing is almost always the
> circumstance that would make it inevitable.

**Reflection.** What circumstance would make your character move at the speed
you have given them?

---

## 5.3 · Two Rhythms at Once

**Objective.** Mark an inner and an outer rhythm on the same speech, and play
the gap between them.

**Orientation.** A person hiding something is running two rhythms at once. The
gap between them is what the audience actually reads.

**Body.**

> Your inner rhythm and your outer one do not have to match, and much of the
> time they should not. Someone waiting for terrible news sits very still.
> Someone lying speaks evenly. The surface is quiet, the interior is racing,
> and the performance lives in the distance between the two.

> The two rhythms also push on each other. The harder you work to hold the
> outside steady, the faster the inside runs, which makes the outside harder
> to hold. That loop is not a problem to solve. It is a thing to play.

> A single mind can split the same way, with resolve running at one speed and
> doubt at another. When a scene feels flat and everything else about it is
> right, the likely cause is that you have given the character one rhythm
> where the moment is asking for two.

**Reflection.** Where in your script is your character calmer on the outside
than on the inside?

---

## 5.4 · Harmony, Not Unison

**Objective.** Hear a scene as separate rhythms sounding together.

**Orientation.** Two people in the same rhythm are singing in unison, and a
scene almost never wants that.

**Body.**

> Everyone carries their own rhythm, on stage as in life. When a whole group
> genuinely shares one, that is unison: soldiers in formation, a chorus, a
> crowd seized by a single urge. In those cases the sameness is the point. A
> scene is built differently. A scene is harmony, which means separate lines
> sounding at the same time.

> A chord needs different notes. Two players on the same note are not making a
> chord, they are just making that note louder. What the audience receives
> between two characters is the interval between their rhythms, and nobody
> plays the interval alone. Hold your own line and let the space between you
> do the work.

> Some intervals sit sweetly and some grate, and both of them are music. Two
> rhythms that refuse to settle are not a scene going wrong. The thing to
> watch for is unison arrived at by accident: a scene rehearsed until everyone
> has quietly drifted onto one pace. It will sound tidy, and it will sound
> nothing like people.

**Reflection.** In this scene, are you in harmony with the other character or
in unison? If it is unison, did you choose it?

---

## 5.5 · Notes and Rests

**Objective.** Treat pauses as part of the rhythm, not gaps in it.

**Orientation.** Syllables are the notes. Pauses are the rests. A rest is
written into the music; it is not an absence of music.

**Body.**

> Speech divides time. Words fill some of it, pauses fill the rest, and both
> of them carry the rhythm. Long sounds and short ones, strong stresses and
> weak ones: that small toolkit builds every rhythm speech has.

> The common failure is the dead pause, a silence that drops the rhythm
> completely. The actor loses the thread, the audience loses it with them, and
> the line has to restart cold. The repair is simple to say and takes practice
> to do: keep a silent count running underneath the pause. The rhythm
> continues. Only the sound stops.

> That same count is what carries you between phrases of different rhythm.
> Prose mixes its rhythms by nature, one phrase long and the next one short,
> each with a pattern of its own. Do not force them into a single measure.
> Keep the count going across the join and let it bridge them.

**Reflection.** Which of your pauses are carrying something? Which are just
waiting?

---

## 5.6 · The Two Missing Speeds

**Objective.** Build true slow speech and true fast speech.

**Orientation.** Most speech on a stage is neither slow nor fast. It is long
pauses with the words rushed out between them.

**Body.**

> Real slowness is hard because slow tends to go dead. The words drift apart,
> the line stalls and restarts, and the audience starts hearing the machinery.
> What keeps a slow line alive is sound rather than silence: let the vowels
> sustain, and let the line keep sounding through its whole length.

> Real speed is a separate discipline, and it is not rushing. Fast speech is
> built out of exaggerated slowness. Take the passage far slower than you will
> ever perform it, with every consonant fully finished, and repeat it until
> your mouth knows the words without your help. Only then do you accelerate.
> Speed reached any other way turns to mush at exactly the moment you most
> need to be understood.

> Both speeds have to stay motivated. A slow line with no reason behind it
> sounds pompous, and a fast line with no reason behind it is a trick. The
> circumstance comes first and the speed follows from it. That order never
> reverses.

**Reflection.** Can you say your hardest line at half speed without it dying,
and at double speed without it turning to mush?
