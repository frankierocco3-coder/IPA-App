# Oral Posture & Articulation: From Rest to Speech

**Speechcraft · Voice & Speech**  
**Key takeaway:** Don’t hold a pose. Make the sound, then move to the next one.

Clear speech comes from a mouth that can change shape easily and precisely. The vocal folds supply the buzz in voiced sounds; the throat, mouth, and nasal cavities shape it. The tongue, jaw, lips, and soft palate also create the closures and narrow openings that distinguish consonants. Changing those shapes can change both the quality of your voice and how easily a listener understands your words. Those are related goals, but not the same one. [NIDCD: How the body produces voice and speech](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/news/multimedia/how-does-human-body-produce-voice-and-speech-text-version)

## Three meanings of oral posture

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Resting posture** | Where the tongue, jaw, and lips settle when you aren’t speaking. It matters in some breathing, comfort, and clinical contexts, but isn’t a position to hold while talking. |
| **Articulatory setting** | The habitual configuration a speaker tends to use when preparing to speak or pausing within speech. Think of it as a starting tendency, not a fixed pose. |
| **Articulation** | The movements and shapes that create sounds as you speak. This is what you practise when you work on a vowel, a consonant, or a line of text. |

Real-time MRI research distinguishes quiet rest from speech preparation and pauses in speech. A small English–French study also found differences between the groups it examined. For accent work, explore a speech-ready setting by listening and experimenting; don’t assume everyone who speaks an accent uses the same tongue position. [Real-time MRI study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3724797/) · [English–French study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15824488/)

## What moves, and what does it change?

| Part | Role | Try noticing it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tongue tip and blade** | Make contacts and narrow passages near the teeth and the ridge behind the upper teeth. | **Tea, sea, lee:** similar neighborhood, different airflow. |
| **Tongue body and back** | Shape vowels and form back closures for sounds like /k/, /ɡ/, and /ŋ/. | **See–ah** for vowel shape; **tea–key** for front versus back closure. |
| **Tongue root and throat** | Help shape the space farther back in the vocal tract. | Listen for the sound change rather than trying to manipulate a part you can’t easily isolate. |
| **Jaw** | Changes mouth opening and gives the tongue and lower lip a moving base. | **See–sat–see:** let the jaw respond to the vowels. |
| **Lips** | Close for /p, b, m/, round for vowels such as /u/, and work with the upper teeth for /f, v/. | **Fee, vee, bee, moo.** |
| **Teeth, hard palate, and alveolar ridge** | Provide landmarks for the tongue or lips. The alveolar ridge is just behind the upper front teeth. | **Thin–sin:** notice the difference between the two constrictions. |
| **Soft palate (velum) and throat walls** | Control how much the nasal cavity participates in the sound. | **Bad–mad:** both start with closed lips, but /m/ allows air through the nose. |

These are typical English examples, not a placement prescription for every speaker. In a jaw-perturbation experiment, speakers compensated with their lips for /b/ but with their tongue for /z/. The practical lesson: the parts coordinate to accomplish a sound; they don’t have to move identically from speaker to speaker. [Interactive IPA chart](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/IPAcharts/IPA_charts_TI/IPA_charts_TI.html) · [Jaw coordination experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6239907/)

## How mouth shape changes the voice

**Pitch is not resonance.** Say *ee–ah–oo* gently at about the same pitch. You can hear three vowels without intentionally changing notes. The vocal folds supply the source; the changing vocal tract acts as a filter. The two can interact, but the distinction is useful when you’re listening. [NIDCD: Voice anatomy](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/taking-care-your-voice) · [Titze: Source–filter interaction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18529191/)

- **F0** is the fundamental frequency, the main acoustic correlate of pitch.
- **F1 and F2** are the first two major vocal-tract resonances, or formants. They help distinguish vowels.
- More open vowels generally have higher F1. More front vowels generally have higher F2, but rounding also affects F2. A formant isn’t a direct reading of tongue position. [MIT acoustic phonetics notes](https://ocw.tau.edu.ng/courses/linguistics-and-philosophy/24-901-language-and-its-structure-i-phonology-fall-2010/lecture-notes/MIT24_901F10_lec08.pdf)

**Nasality is not the same as brightness.** Nasal resonance is needed for /m, n, ŋ/, and some neighboring vowel nasalization can be normal. Too little nasal resonance can sound congested; excessive nasal resonance and nasal air escaping on pressure consonants are related but distinct phenomena. A buzz felt near the face doesn’t diagnose either one. Don’t try to hold the soft palate up throughout speech. [ASHA: Resonance Disorders](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/resonance-disorders/)

**American R doesn’t have one mandatory shape.** For /ɹ/, some speakers raise the tongue tip; others bunch the tongue body with a lower tip. Some vary across contexts. In *R is everywhere*, show more than one option and use a listening goal rather than prescribing a curled tip. [Ultrasound biofeedback study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25088034/)

## How to enunciate without sounding mechanical

Enunciation is about helping the listener distinguish words. That depends on articulation, timing, emphasis, phrasing, and audibility. Speech gestures naturally overlap: the lips can start rounding for a vowel before the previous consonant is finished. This is **coarticulation**, not sloppiness. You don’t need to reset the mouth between sounds. [Study of anticipatory lip rounding](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/jshr.1104.707)

Clear-speech research shows that making sounds more distinct can improve intelligibility, and that clear speech is possible at a normal rate. Brief exaggeration can help you discover a contrast; then bring it back into the line. [Lam and Tjaden: Clear-speech instructions](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23798509/) · [Krause and Braida: Clear speech and speaking rate](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12430828/)

- **Make the meaningful contrast audible.** In “I said *coat*, not *goat*,” focus on the initial consonants.
- **Keep meaningful endings distinct.** Compare “She *packs*” and “She *packed*.” Don’t force a puff of air onto every final consonant.
- **Stress the thought.** Say “I didn’t say she took it,” emphasizing **I**, then **say**, then **she**. The same words communicate different corrections.
- **Pause between ideas**, not between every word.
- **Keep accent and clarity separate.** An accent is a sound pattern, not a defect. Choose a target that serves the audience or the character. [ASHA: Articulation and phonology](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/articulation-and-phonology/)

## What not to teach as a universal rule

- **“Press your whole tongue to the roof of your mouth.”** Resting posture can be a clinical target for some people, but holding that pose is not a general diction technique. The American Association of Orthodontists also says the facial-reshaping claims made for *mewing* lack scientific support. [ASHA: Orofacial Myofunctional Disorders](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/orofacial-myofunctional-disorders/) · [AAO: Mewing](https://aaoinfo.org/whats-trending/is-mewing-bad-for-you/)
- **“Relax by dropping the jaw and making the tongue limp.”** Aim for less unnecessary effort, not zero activity. Let the jaw move with the task. [Speech coordination research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12173160/)
- **“Mumbling means your tongue is weak.”** Don’t infer weakness from sound alone. A Cochrane review found no strong evidence for nonspeech oral-motor exercises in children with developmental speech-sound disorders. That does not settle every question about adult training or rehabilitation. For an ordinary clarity goal, practise the actual sounds and phrases. [Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD009383_non-speech-oral-motor-treatment-children-developmental-speech-sound-disorders)
- **“Never breathe through your mouth.”** Quiet resting breathing and breathing between lines are different tasks. Persistent nasal obstruction is worth assessing; don’t force the mouth closed to override it. [ASHA: Oral posture and airway](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/orofacial-myofunctional-disorders/)
- **“Straw phonation is a tongue-strengthening or diction drill.”** Voicing through a straw changes airflow and acoustic conditions during phonation. It’s a voice exercise, not a replacement for practising a consonant or a phrase. [Titze: Semi-occluded vocal tract exercises](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16671856/) · [Randomized voice-therapy trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25675335/)

## Five-minute actor’s experiment

Use a comfortable speaking voice. These are observation and performance exercises, not treatment for pain or a speech disorder.

1. **Record a baseline.** Say, “I asked for the blue coat, not the black one.” Speak to an imagined person, not to the microphone.
2. **Hear the filter.** Say *ee–ah–oo* gently at roughly the same pitch. Notice how the sound changes without trying to freeze your jaw, tongue, or lips.
3. **Locate the closure.** Say *sum–sun–sung*. Notice how the closure moves from the lips to the front of the tongue to the back of the tongue.
4. **Change one thing.** Give *blue* and *black* a clearer contrast without increasing volume. In another take, try a small pause after *coat*. Compare the changes separately.
5. **Return to the scene.** Say the line at a natural pace with the intention intact. Listen back or ask someone who hasn’t seen the text what they heard. Keep the adjustment that made the thought clearer without adding effort.

## When to get individual help

A speech-language pathologist can assess persistent difficulty with particular sounds or with intelligibility. Persistent jaw pain, locking, or nasal obstruction warrant their own assessment. For hoarseness or voice change that hasn’t improved within four weeks, the otolaryngology guideline recommends examination of the larynx, sooner if a serious cause is suspected. Professional voice use can also warrant earlier evaluation. [ASHA: Speech-sound assessment](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/articulation-and-phonology/) · [Clinical guideline on hoarseness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29494321/)

## Suggested Speechcraft lesson flow

1. Label a side-view illustration: lips, teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, soft palate, jaw, tongue tip/body/root, and vocal folds.
2. Compare recordings of the same pitch on *ee–ah–oo*. Ask what changed and what stayed roughly the same.
3. Compare the closure locations in *sum–sun–sung* and the airflow in *bad–mad*.
4. Show two possible American R shapes and connect to *R is everywhere*.
5. Finish with the actor’s line experiment. Ask the learner to name one change that helped the listener, rather than score their accent or resting posture.
