# Words first recorded in Shakespeare — the candidate lexicon

Research intake, 2026-10-14. **Not course content, and not importable.** Nothing
here may be stated to a learner as a word Shakespeare invented until it has
been checked individually, for the reasons below.

## The thing that does not exist

There is no lexicon of words Shakespeare invented, and the obstacle is not
effort. Every list of this kind is a list of FIRST CITATIONS — the earliest
surviving printed use a dictionary's readers happened to find. The Oxford
English Dictionary says so itself, in the Preface to its Third Edition: a
first citation means only that no earlier evidence has yet come to light, and
printed evidence usually does not record a word's first-ever use.

Four totals are in circulation and not one of them counts inventions:

| Figure | Source | What it actually counts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ~3,200 | Alfred Hart, early 1940s | OED first citations |
| ~2,035 | OED, 2nd edition | words with Shakespeare as earliest recorded user |
| ~1,700 | David Crystal, 2004 | an ESTIMATE of probable coinages, derived from the 2,035 by judgement |
| 1,467 | OED, 3rd edition, June 2019 | first citations surviving revision, STILL FALLING |
| 594 | ElizabethanDrama.org | individual words, compounds excluded |

**The famous "1,700 invented words" is not an OED count.** It is Crystal's
estimate, reached by taking the 2,035 and judging how many were likely to be
genuine coinages. Sources that report it as "the words whose earliest OED
citation is Shakespeare" have swapped one quantity for another.

**The number keeps falling because the evidence keeps improving.** Over the one
stretch of alphabet where OED2 and OED3 can be compared directly, Shakespeare's
first-use entries dropped from 406 to 263 — earlier uses turned up for roughly a
third of them. Searchable databases of early printed books did that, and the
revision is incomplete.

**The dictionary was always going to over-count him.** Murray's reading
programme was canonical by design and Shakespeare is the OED's most-quoted
source at roughly 33,000 quotations, against about 15,000 for the next
individual author. Correct for that and his rate of lexical innovation is, in
Jonathan Hope's phrase, "strictly average" for a period when almost every writer
was coining words. (The claim that the Victorian editors KNEW they were
over-quoting him was checked and does not hold; it inverts the record.)

## What this list is made of

590 of the 594 forms were analysed; the four omitted are multi-word entries
(`hic jacet`, `memento mori`) and slash variants collapsed to one form.

```
built with a standard prefix    250   42%
  un-  98 (17%)   in- 28   co- 22   en- 18   re- 18   be- 15   dis- 15
built with a standard suffix
  -ed 101   -y 45   -less 32   -ing 30   -ment 24   -ly 23
```

**Over forty per cent is a prefix bolted onto a word that already existed**, and
`un-` alone is nearly a fifth: *unaching, unbloodied, unbonneted, uncuckolded,
unshrubbed, unwedgeable*. In any other author nobody would call that invention.
It is ordinary English morphology used freely, which is exactly what "strictly
average for the period" means in practice.

**33 records are sense-qualified** — `end (verb 2)`, `evil (noun 2)`, `push
(noun 3)`, `impress (noun 2 and verb 1)`. Those are not new words at all but new
MEANINGS for existing ones, and conflating the two is how popular counts inflate.
The OED credits him with far more sense-level first citations than word-level
ones. The source schema separates them, which most do not.

**4 records carry shared credit**: *priceless, sympathize, unclog, uncurse*.

## The vast majority died immediately

*a-billing, anthropophaginian, apathaton, bubukle, cubiculo,
disinsanity, empiricutic, exsufflicate, fustilarian, kickie-wickie, pibble-pabble,
skimble-skamble, vizament* — these are nonce words, coined for one line and never
used again by anyone. A handful did survive into ordinary English, and those are
the only ones worth a learner's attention. Identifying them reliably needs a
frequency corpus, which this repository does not have; a judgement by eye would
be opinion dressed as measurement, and is deliberately not recorded here.

## What it would take to make this course content

1. Each candidate checked against current OED3 evidence, since a third of the
   ones rechecked so far have been antedated.
2. Word-level and sense-level claims kept apart.
3. The surviving-into-modern-English subset identified against a frequency
   corpus, not by eye.
4. A reviewer qualified to sign it off, as with the existing lexicon shelf —
   which is badged awaiting a Shakespeare scholar or verse teacher for exactly
   this class of claim.

Until then the honest framing for anything built from this is **"words first
recorded in Shakespeare"**, never "words Shakespeare invented". The first is
checkable. The second is not known, and the scholarship has been moving away
from it for twenty years.

# The glossed lexicon

Meanings AS THE WORD IS USED IN SHAKESPEARE, which is not always what it came to
mean later. Written 2026-10-14.

**These are original definitions and nobody qualified has checked them.** They
carry the status the 277-entry Shakespeare Dictionary carries: awaiting review by
a Shakespeare scholar or verse teacher. A definition of Shakespeare's usage is a
scholarly claim, and that belongs in plain sight rather than in a footnote.

No play references are given. A good number could be placed from memory, and
memory is not a source; an empty column is better than one that looks checked
and is not.

Markers: `[lives]` still in ordinary English · `[died]` used once or twice and
gone · `[sense]` the word existed, the new thing is this meaning · `?` low
confidence, a reviewer should look hard.

## PART ONE — the ones worth knowing

Two kinds of word earn a place here. The ones that **survived**, which an actor
meets and already half-knows. And the ones that **died but play**, which an actor
hits in a text and needs, usually insults and comic bombast. Everything else —
`a-hooting` meaning hooting — is in Part Two and teaches nobody anything.

**A whole class of these are jokes about people who cannot use words.**
`allicholly`, `apathaton`, `argal`, `vizament`, `tirrit` are characters getting
a word wrong. Crediting Shakespeare with inventing them is like crediting him
with inventing the malapropism: it is characterisation, not vocabulary.
